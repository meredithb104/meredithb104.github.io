The year was 2015, and I was in my first semester of college. If you remember, some political happenings were brewing in 2015. I received many Democratic fundraising emails, frequently begging me for $5. For just $5, I could win a trip to the White House to meet President Obama, a chance as tantalizing to a 2015 liberal as the Danimals yogurt sweepstakes to meet Zack and Cody from *The Suite Life* had once been. I was broke and did not donate, so when yet another email from "President Obama" arrived, I ignored it. In fact, I ignored five to seven emails telling me that I had won a prize to go to the White House. Then the phone calls started, from DC area codes and unlisted numbers. The callers left neither voicemails nor callback numbers until one morning in French class, when I received two calls in a row. The second caller left a voicemail about my having won something, along with a phone number and a warning: I should call back from a landline, or I might forfeit the mystery prize.

I returned to class very confused, and my French professor demanded that I explain my absence, *en français*. I tried to explain in butchered French, but all I remember saying is "La maison blanche, je besoin d'une telephone landline" (The White House, I need a landline telephone). After class, I sprinted to the front office of my tiny liberal arts college, because I didn't know where else to find a landline.

My memories are a bit of a blur from that point. I called the number and told the woman who answered at the White House that I would accept. Then she told me that the award would be presented at a press conference in a week and that I needed to attend in person. I panicked. I had no easy way to get to Washington. I was blind and broke, and my mother couldn't just take off work; she doesn't work for the love of it, either. Without someone to guide me, I had no way to make the trip at all. I also needed a haircut.

Excitement gave way to anxiety and fear. I called my mother first, then my new roommate. Mama was speechless, but my roommate came up with a partial solution: her father donated his frequent-flyer miles and hotel points so that my mother, my roommate, and I could travel to Washington to accept the award. Robert, my father's barber, gave me the haircut for free. Ruthe Farmer, then of NCWIT, offered me some very sound advice: embrace any media attention the award brought, and use it as a platform to promote accessibility for disabled students in STEM education. Few advocates were working on that frontier of STEM equity in 2015, and fewer still were the students living with its consequences.

Local media outlets interviewed me before the trip. The interviews took place in the front room of Wilson Hall at Converse College, and I drew on my own experience as a multiply disabled student, using every skill I had learned in speech therapy to advocate for accessibility in K–12 public education for all disabled students. It felt like a series of IEP meetings, but this time it wasn't only about me. It was about every student with a disability who had been relegated to the sidelines of a classroom. I was proud of myself for handling the press with minimal stuttering and apraxia of speech.

On the day of the award ceremony and speaker panel, I confused the Secret Service agents by bringing a Chia Pet bust of President Obama to the event. I also gave the most awkward hug ever to Valerie Jarrett, senior advisor to the president, who filled in for the president at the panels that day because he was meeting with the king of Spain. She told me that she liked my chevron dress. Fair play. I didn't get to meet President Obama, but I did get to spend time with my mentor, Ruthe, who had secretly nominated me for the White House Champions of Change program. Ruthe is now the CEO of the Last Mile Education Fund, where she continues to empower and inspire young people in higher education.

Ruthe saw something in me before I saw it in myself, and she made sure that I had the advice, the connections, and the confidence to make the most of that moment. I have wanted to pay it forward ever since. This summer, I joined the ND Project, a student-led organization for neurodiverse, disabled, and chronically ill students in higher education, as a mentor. The students run the show. My job is to do for them what Ruthe did for me: open doors, offer sound advice when they want it, and occasionally nominate them for something without telling them first.

I haven't donated $5 to the Democratic Party yet, but there is still time.
